👋 Introduction to Real Numbers

Why does this chapter matter? Real Numbers is Chapter 1 of CBSE Class 10 Maths and carries 6–8 marks in the board exam every year. Mastering Euclid's Division Algorithm and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic will make number theory questions feel effortless. Let's get started!

The chapter Real Numbers builds on your Class 9 knowledge of the number system. You'll learn powerful tools for finding HCF and LCM without listing all factors, prove that certain numbers like √2 and √3 are irrational, and understand the building blocks of all integers. These concepts appear not just in Class 10, but also in JEE Foundation and Olympiad problems.

🔢 1. Euclid's Division Lemma (EDL)
a = b × q + r    where   0 ≤ r < b
Where: a = dividend, b = divisor, q = quotient, r = remainder
Key point: For any two positive integers a and b, there exist unique whole numbers q and r satisfying this relation. The remainder r is always non-negative and less than b.
🔍 2. Euclid's Division Algorithm (EDA) — HCF
HCF(a, b) → Apply EDL repeatedly until r = 0
Last non-zero remainder = HCF
Steps: (1) Divide a by b, get remainder r₁. (2) Now divide b by r₁, get r₂. (3) Continue until remainder = 0. The divisor at that step is HCF.
Example: HCF(135, 225) → 225 = 135×1 + 90 → 135 = 90×1 + 45 → 90 = 45×2 + 0 ∴ HCF = 45
🏗️ 3. Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
Every composite number = Product of primes (unique)
Every integer greater than 1 is either prime or can be expressed as a unique product of prime numbers (order doesn't matter). This is also called Prime Factorisation.
Example: 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5
🔗 4. HCF and LCM Relationship
HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
Valid for: Any two positive integers a and b.
HCF from prime factorisation: Take lowest power of each common prime factor.
LCM from prime factorisation: Take highest power of each prime factor (common or not).
Example: a = 12, b = 18 → HCF = 6, LCM = 36 → 6 × 36 = 216 = 12 × 18 ✓
🌀 5. Irrational Numbers — Proof Technique
If p is prime and p | a², then p | a
This is the key lemma used to prove √2, √3, √5, etc. are irrational. The method is Proof by Contradiction.
Steps for proving √2 is irrational:
(1) Assume √2 = p/q (in lowest terms). (2) Then 2q² = p², so 2 | p². (3) By lemma, 2 | p, so p = 2m. (4) Then 2q² = 4m² → q² = 2m², so 2 | q. (5) Both p and q divisible by 2 — contradicts "lowest terms". ∴ √2 is irrational.
📊 6. Decimal Expansions of Rational Numbers
p/q terminates ↔ q = 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ (n, m ≥ 0)
A rational number p/q (in lowest terms) has a terminating decimal if and only if its denominator q has no prime factor other than 2 and 5.
Terminating: 7/8 = 7/2³ → 0.875 ✓
Non-terminating repeating: 1/3 = 0.333... (denominator has factor 3) ✗
Irrational: Non-terminating, non-repeating (e.g., π, √2)

📜 Key Theorems to Remember

Theorem 1
Euclid's Division Lemma
Given positive integers a and b, there exist unique integers q and r such that:
a = b × q + r    (0 ≤ r < b)
This guarantees the uniqueness of quotient and remainder in division.
Theorem 2
Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
Every composite number can be factorised as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique apart from the order of the prime factors.
n = p₁ᵃ × p₂ᵇ × p₃ᶜ × ... (where p₁, p₂, p₃ ... are primes)
Theorem 3
Irrationality of √p (where p is prime)
If p is a prime number, then √p is irrational. This is proven by contradiction using the key lemma:
If p | a², then p | a    (for prime p)
Theorem 4
Terminating Decimal Condition
Let p/q be a rational number in lowest terms. Its decimal expansion terminates if and only if q is of the form:
q = 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ    (n, m are non-negative integers)

📝 Important Questions & Solutions

Q1. Use Euclid's division algorithm to find HCF of 867 and 255.
2 marks+
Solution:
Step 1: 867 = 255 × 3 + 102
Step 2: 255 = 102 × 2 + 51
Step 3: 102 = 51 × 2 + 0

Since remainder = 0, HCF(867, 255) = 51
Q2. Prove that √5 is irrational.
3 marks+
Proof by Contradiction:
Assume √5 is rational → √5 = a/b (in lowest terms, HCF(a,b) = 1)
→ 5 = a²/b² → a² = 5b² → 5 | a² → 5 | a (by key lemma)
Let a = 5c → a² = 25c² → 5b² = 25c² → b² = 5c² → 5 | b
Both a and b are divisible by 5. This contradicts HCF(a,b) = 1.
∴ Our assumption was wrong. √5 is irrational. □
Q3. Find HCF and LCM of 96 and 404 by prime factorisation. Verify HCF × LCM = a × b.
3 marks+
96 = 2⁵ × 3   |   404 = 2² × 101

HCF = 2² = 4
LCM = 2⁵ × 3 × 101 = 9696

Verification: HCF × LCM = 4 × 9696 = 38784
a × b = 96 × 404 = 38784 ✓
Q4. Without actually performing the long division, state whether 17/8 has a terminating or non-terminating decimal expansion.
1 mark+
Denominator = 8 = 2³. Since 8 = 2³ × 5⁰ (form 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ), the decimal expansion of 17/8 is terminating.
17/8 = 2.125
Q5. Prove that 3 + 2√5 is irrational.
3 marks+
Assume 3 + 2√5 is rational. Then 3 + 2√5 = a/b (where a, b are integers, b ≠ 0).
→ 2√5 = a/b − 3 = (a − 3b)/b
→ √5 = (a − 3b)/(2b)
Since a, b are integers, (a − 3b)/(2b) is rational → √5 is rational.
But this contradicts the fact that √5 is irrational.
∴ 3 + 2√5 is irrational. □
Q6. The HCF of two numbers is 23 and their LCM is 1449. If one number is 161, find the other.
2 marks+
Using: HCF × LCM = a × b
23 × 1449 = 161 × b
b = (23 × 1449)/161 = 33327/161 = 207

⚠️ Common Mistakes Students Make

❌ Wrong
HCF × LCM = a × b applies to THREE numbers too
✅ Correct
HCF × LCM = a × b is valid ONLY for TWO numbers
❌ Wrong
Writing LCM as product of lowest powers of prime factors
✅ Correct
LCM = product of HIGHEST powers; HCF = LOWEST powers
❌ Wrong
Stopping Euclid's algorithm when q = 0 (quotient)
✅ Correct
Stop when REMAINDER r = 0; the divisor at that step is HCF
❌ Wrong
1/7 has terminating decimal (7 is not 2 or 5, but ignoring)
✅ Correct
1/7 = 0.142857... non-terminating (7 ≠ 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ form)
❌ Wrong
In proof of irrationality, forgetting to state "HCF(a,b) = 1"
✅ Correct
Always assume p/q in lowest terms (i.e., HCF = 1) at start of proof

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is Euclid's Division Lemma in Class 10 Maths?
Euclid's Division Lemma states that for any two positive integers a and b, there exist unique whole numbers q (quotient) and r (remainder) such that: a = b × q + r, where 0 ≤ r < b. It is the foundation of Euclid's Division Algorithm used to find HCF.
What is the difference between HCF and LCM in prime factorisation?
In prime factorisation method: HCF = product of the LOWEST (minimum) powers of all COMMON prime factors. LCM = product of the HIGHEST (maximum) powers of ALL prime factors (common and non-common). And always: HCF × LCM = Product of the two numbers.
How do I prove a number is irrational in Class 10?
Use Proof by Contradiction: (1) Assume the number is rational, i.e., equal to p/q in lowest terms (HCF = 1). (2) Square both sides and manipulate to show a prime divides both p and q. (3) This contradicts HCF(p,q) = 1. Therefore the number must be irrational. This method works for √2, √3, √5 and their combinations.
How to check if a rational number has a terminating decimal?
Reduce the fraction p/q to its lowest terms. Then check the denominator q. If q can be written as 2ⁿ × 5ᵐ (only powers of 2 and 5), the decimal is terminating. If q has any other prime factor (like 3, 7, 11, etc.), the decimal is non-terminating repeating.
Can HCF be greater than LCM?
No! HCF is always less than or equal to LCM. HCF = LCM only when both numbers are equal. For different numbers, HCF < LCM always. Also, HCF always divides LCM exactly.
Is π (pi) a rational or irrational number?
π is an irrational number. Its decimal expansion is non-terminating and non-repeating (3.14159265...). Note that 22/7 is only an approximation of π — it is NOT equal to π. 22/7 ≈ 3.142857... while π ≈ 3.14159...
How many marks does Real Numbers carry in CBSE Class 10 board exam?
Chapter 1 Real Numbers typically carries 6 to 8 marks in the CBSE Class 10 Maths board paper. This usually includes one 1-mark MCQ, one 2-mark question (HCF by algorithm), and one 3-mark question (either LCM+HCF by factorisation or an irrationality proof).
What is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic?
The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic states that every composite number greater than 1 can be expressed as a product of prime numbers in a unique way (ignoring order). For example: 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5. This uniqueness is what makes prime factorisation so powerful for finding HCF and LCM.
